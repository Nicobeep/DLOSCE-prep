# Restorative dentistry
**Weight: 24% · Official domains:** diagnosis, preparations, direct restorations, indirect restorations.

## 1. Caries diagnosis and treatment threshold
- **Caries risk and lesion activity matter more than the X-ray alone.** Collect diet, fluoride exposure, saliva, plaque, caries history, and disease indicators. Visual-tactile findings and previous films help assess progression.
- **Enamel-only, noncavitated lesion:** favor remineralization, fluoride, behavior modification, and monitoring; resin infiltration/sealants may be options for appropriately selected lesions.
- **Cavitated or noncleansable lesions:** restorative intervention is more likely when biofilm cannot be controlled or structure is compromised. Preserve sound tissue.
- **Radiographic radiolucency ≠ a cavitated surface.** Proximal radiographs often underestimate lesion extent; assess clinical surface integrity.
- **Secondary/recurrent caries:** look for an active lesion and identifiable defective margin; a stain or marginal discoloration alone is not an indication to replace the restoration.
- **Cervical burnout** can mimic proximal root caries. Consider location, radiographic geometry, and confirmatory clinical assessment.
- **Deep caries:** prioritize pulpal preservation when appropriate and plan selective caries removal or vital pulp therapy based on pulp diagnosis, symptoms, exposure and restorability.

## 2. Direct restorative procedures
- **Class I:** pits and fissures. **II:** posterior proximal. **III:** anterior proximal without incisal angle. **IV:** anterior proximal with incisal angle. **V:** cervical third of facial/lingual. **VI:** cusp tips/incisal edges.
- **Matrix + wedge:** establish proximal contour/contact, help prevent gingival overhang, separate teeth as needed, and seal the gingival margin.
- **Composite** is technique-sensitive: reliable isolation, proper enamel/dentin adhesion, controlled placement and curing, and correct proximal contact are critical.
- **Glass ionomer** chemically bonds and releases fluoride; moisture handling and strength differ from resin composite. **Resin-modified glass ionomer** offers additional resin properties.
- **Amalgam** depends more on mechanical preparation/retention and bulk; know fracture-risk, unsupported enamel and marginal deterioration concepts.
- **Postoperative sensitivity differential:** high occlusion, exposed dentin, polymerization stress, microleakage, pulp inflammation, or cracked tooth. Do not reflexively prescribe antibiotics.
- **Repair vs replacement:** consider localized defect, active disease, existing restoration size, risk of additional removal, and ability to correct the defect predictably.

## 3. Indirect restorations and tooth structure
- Choose **onlay/cuspal coverage** when weakened cusps or extensive MOD compromise make fracture risk clinically important.
- **Full crown is not automatically required after root canal therapy.** Evaluate tooth type, remaining walls, ferrule, occlusion, cracks, and periodontal status.
- **Retention** resists displacement along the path of insertion; **resistance** resists tipping/oblique forces. Short, over-tapered preparations lose resistance.
- Maintain adequate **occlusal/axial reduction** for material thickness; avoid under-reduction, over-taper, rough irregular finish lines, and sharp internal line angles where inappropriate.
- **Ferrule** refers to a circumferential collar of sound tooth structure engaged by the final restoration; it improves resistance to functional fracture in many endodontically treated teeth.
- **Fixed prosthodontics crossover:** assess biologic width/supracrestal tissue attachment, finish-line location, margins, proximal contacts, emergence profile, and hygiene access.
- **Cracked tooth:** biting/release pain and localized periodontal defects may indicate crack depth. A vertical root fracture may carry poor prognosis.

## 4. Decision pathway (the way cases are tested)
1. Is the lesion **active**, **cavitated**, and **cleansable**? Assess overall caries risk.
2. Does the tooth have **reversible, irreversible or necrotic pulpal disease**? Test before definitive restoration.
3. Is there enough tooth to retain a direct restoration, or are **cusps/remaining walls compromised**?
4. Can the restoration be isolated, contoured and cleaned? Are periodontal and occlusal conditions favorable?
5. Choose the **least invasive predictable** option with follow-up.

## 5. Common distractors and traps
- Visible discoloration alone → **not** necessarily recurrent caries.
- Large radiolucency near the pulp → **does not** establish pulp necrosis; test.
- Every fractured restoration → **not** automatically a crown.
- Every deep lesion → **not** automatic root canal; diagnosis and vital pulp options matter.
- Lingering thermal pain → consider pulpal diagnosis before final restoration.
- Overhangs and open contacts promote plaque retention/food impaction; consider gingival health.

## Rapid recall
- **Caries in noncavitated outer enamel?** Nonoperative control + reassessment first, if appropriate.
- **Class IV?** Anterior proximal involving incisal angle.
- **Loss of two marginal ridges/cusps?** Evaluate cuspal protection.
- **High postoperative bite?** Check occlusion and other causes of sensitivity.
- **Stained but intact margin?** Determine if active lesion/defect exists before replacement.
