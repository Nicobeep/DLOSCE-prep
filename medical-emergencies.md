# Medical emergencies
**Weight: 6% · Official domains:** diagnosis, management.

## 1. First response sequence
- **Stop treatment.** Check responsiveness and airway, breathing and circulation; call trained team and activate EMS when appropriate.
- Initiate **BLS/CPR/AED** per current American Heart Association guidance for unresponsive patients without normal breathing.
- Provide oxygen and monitoring according to clinical condition, equipment and emergency protocol; do not delay definitive emergency actions.
- Know clinic emergency kit, emergency phone access, staff roles and patient transfer procedures.
- **Prevention:** updated health history, medication/allergy review, vital signs, patient positioning, anxiety control and appropriate medical consultation.

## 2. Recognize time-sensitive emergencies
- **Anaphylaxis:** sudden rash/angioedema with respiratory compromise or hypotension after exposure → **IM epinephrine is first-line**, call EMS, monitor and support airway.
- **Hypoglycemia:** sweating, tremor, confusion, behavior change, seizure or unconsciousness → check glucose if feasible; conscious and safely swallowing patient can receive oral glucose; unconscious patient must not be given oral liquids.
- **Acute coronary syndrome:** pressure/heaviness, dyspnea, diaphoresis, radiation or atypical symptoms → stop care, activate emergency response and follow protocols; do not assume all chest pain is anxiety.
- **Stroke:** facial droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty, sudden neurologic deficit → call EMS promptly, document time last known well.
- **Seizure:** protect from injury, time duration, maintain airway after episode; emergency escalation for prolonged/repeated seizures or compromise.
- **Asthma exacerbation:** wheezing, dyspnea; use prescribed rescue bronchodilator as appropriate; escalate if severe or not responding.
- **Local anesthetic systemic toxicity:** CNS symptoms (tinnitus, metallic taste, perioral numbness, agitation/seizures) and/or cardiac toxicity → stop injection and follow emergency protocol; intravenous lipid therapy is administered by trained responders in severe cases.

## 3. Syncope vs other causes
- **Vasovagal syncope** often follows stress/procedure with pallor, sweating, bradycardia and rapid recovery in appropriate positioning.
- Do not label prolonged altered mental status as simple syncope without checking **glucose, oxygenation, neurologic status and cardiac symptoms**.
- **Hyperventilation** can cause paresthesia/lightheadedness but is a diagnosis after excluding dangerous causes; provide calm coaching and assess vitals.
- Orthostatic issues, dehydration and medications may contribute to presyncope.

## 4. Safety traps
- **Antihistamines are not first-line treatment for anaphylaxis.**
- **Do not give oral glucose to an unconscious patient** due to aspiration risk.
- **Do not place objects in the mouth during a seizure.**
- **Do not delay EMS** for suspected stroke, MI, severe anaphylaxis or airway compromise.
- **BLS algorithms change**: verify current AHA recommendations and local training, not memorized old numbers alone.

## Rapid recall
- **Anaphylaxis first-line medication?** Intramuscular epinephrine.
- **FAST-positive patient?** EMS activation and record last-known-well time.
- **Unconscious hypoglycemic?** No oral intake; emergency management.
- **Seizing patient?** Protect, time event, assess airway, escalate when indicated.
- **Unresponsive, not normally breathing?** Begin BLS/CPR/AED response.
